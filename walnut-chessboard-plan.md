# Walnut & White Wood Chessboard — Build Plan

Circular saw + straightedge build (no table saw). Method: strip-glue checkerboard, glued to a plywood backer, wrapped in a mitered border.

## The concept

Since your walnut and white 1x2s already come milled to a consistent width (actual ~1.5"), you don't need to rip anything — only crosscut to length. That's the key move that makes this doable without a table saw. Squares come out at 1.5" x 1.5", giving you a compact 12" x 12" playing surface (a "travel" size board, not tournament size — bigger squares would mean ripping the stock down after gluing two boards together, which is a stretch goal noted at the bottom).

**Verify first:** check that the walnut and white boards are actually the same width with calipers or by butting them edge to edge. Even 1/32" of difference between species will show up as a stair-step in the final grid.

## Wood/material recommendation

Your species choices are already made (walnut, your white wood, and your border wood) — no substitution needed there. The one thing you're missing is the backer:

**Recommend:** 1/2" birch plywood, 12"x12". Cheap, flat, stable, and gives the glued checkerboard something rigid to sit on so the cross-grain glue joints don't crack over time. MDF works too and is even flatter, but is heavier and doesn't hold screws as well if you ever want to add feet.

## Tools needed

- Circular saw
- Straightedge guide (track saw guide, or a clamped board/speed square)
- Speed square (for marking square lines)
- Stop block (a scrap of wood clamped to your guide) — this is what keeps every crosscut identical length
- Wood glue + clamps (bar clamps or a band/strap clamp — you'll need enough reach to clamp a 12" panel)
- Manual miter box + backsaw (for the frame corners — see note below)
- Random orbital sander or sanding block, 120/220 grit
- Tape measure, pencil, calipers (if you have them, to verify board widths)

**On the miter box:** cutting clean 45° corners freehand with a circular saw is genuinely hard and this is the one place in the whole project where bad cuts ruin the look. A $15–20 manual miter box (hand-powered, not an electric miter saw) will get you square, repeatable 45s for the 8 corner cuts on the border. Worth buying just for this step.

## Step-by-step instructions

**Stage 1 — Cut your strips**
1. Set up your straightedge guide with a stop block so every crosscut is exactly 12" without re-measuring each time.
2. Cut 4 strips of walnut at 12" long.
3. Cut 4 strips of white at 12" long.
4. Check every strip against one master strip — they need to match within 1/32" or the grid will drift.

**Stage 2 — Glue the column panel**
5. Lay the 8 strips on edge in alternating order: Walnut–White–Walnut–White–Walnut–White–Walnut–White.
6. Apply glue to the mating edges, clamp the whole panel together (cauls or scrap boards across the top/bottom help keep it flat while clamping), and let it dry fully (at least a few hours, overnight is safer).
7. You now have one 12" x 12" panel with 8 vertical stripes.

**Stage 3 — Crosscut into rows**
8. Once dry, scrape off squeeze-out and crosscut the panel perpendicular to the original strips, into 8 strips, each 1.5" wide. Use your stop block again so every row is identical width.
9. Lay the 8 new strips out in order on your bench, keeping track of which end was "up" on each.
10. Flip every other strip end-for-end (rotate it 180° in place, not face-down). This reverses the color sequence in that strip, which is what turns 8 identical striped rows into an actual checkerboard pattern.

**Stage 4 — Glue the checkerboard**
11. Double check the pattern reads as a proper checkerboard before gluing (32 walnut squares, 32 white squares, no two same-color squares touching).
12. Glue and clamp the 8 rows back together the same way you did the columns. Let dry overnight.
13. Sand the whole face flat and flush — this stage always has some ridge between rows/columns even with careful gluing.

**Stage 5 — Mount to the backer**
14. Cut your plywood/MDF backer to 12" x 12".
15. Glue the checkerboard panel to the backer, weight it down or clamp with cauls, let dry.

**Stage 6 — Build and attach the border**
16. Using your miter box, cut the 4 border pieces at 15" (long point to long point), mitering both ends of each piece at 45°.
17. Dry-fit all 4 corners before gluing anything — trim slightly if any corner gaps.
18. Glue the mitered frame around the backer + checkerboard assembly. The border (1.5" tall) will sit proud above the 3/4" checkerboard surface, creating a shallow raised lip — useful for holding pieces during play.
19. Clamp with a band/strap clamp (best option for wrapping all 4 corners at once) and let dry overnight.

**Stage 7 — Finish**
20. Final sand at 220 grit, working up from the border joints to the playing surface.
21. Apply 2–3 coats of Danish oil or wipe-on poly, per the can's dry-time instructions between coats. Oil finishes bring out the walnut/white contrast best.

## Difficulty & time

**Difficulty:** Intermediate. The technique itself is simple, but precision compounds — a slightly-off crosscut at Stage 1 shows up as a crooked grid by Stage 4. The stop-block method is what keeps this in check.

**Time:** ~5–6 hours of active shop time, spread across 3–4 days to allow glue-ups to dry between stages. Don't rush the drying — clamping the next stage before the last one's fully cured is the most common way this kind of project goes sideways.

## Stretch goal: bigger squares

If 1.5" squares feel too small once you see it in person, the upgrade path is to glue two 1x2 boards edge-to-edge (one walnut, one white, into a 3"-wide blank), let that dry, then rip it down the middle with your straightedge to get two 1.5"-wide strips of *pre-joined* color pairs — or rip to a wider target (2"-2.25") if you're comfortable with a straight rip cut along a guide. A single long rip cut is more forgiving than dozens of short crosscuts, so this is still realistic without a table saw — just treat it as a separate test board first before committing your good stock.
