# Simple Slat Chair — Build Plan

Straight-leg, straight-back chair. No angled cuts, no compound joinery — every cut is a square crosscut doable with a circular saw. Built with butt joints, glue, and screws.

**Finished size:** 16"W x 15"D x 33"H, seat height ~18"
**Difficulty:** Beginner
**Build time:** 3–4 hours cutting/assembly + drying time overnight + finishing

## Cut List

**Legs (from 2x2x8' stock):**
| Piece | Qty | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Front leg | 2 | 17" |
| Back leg | 2 | 33" |

Cut both back legs (33" + 33" = 66") and one front leg (17") from Board 1 — leaves ~13" scrap. Cut the second front leg (17") from Board 2; the ~79" remainder is extra scrap, good for shop shims or a future small project.

**Rails, stretchers, slats, backrest (from 1x4x8' stock):**
| Piece | Qty | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Front/back seat rail | 2 | 13" |
| Side seat rail | 2 | 12" |
| Front/back lower stretcher | 2 | 13" |
| Side lower stretcher | 2 | 12" |
| Seat slat | 3 | 15" |
| Backrest slat | 2 | 13" |

Total linear inches needed: 171" — two 8' (96") boards give you 192", so there's slack for a bad cut.

## Tools Needed

- Circular saw + straightedge guide
- Drill/driver with pilot bit and countersink
- Sander (orbital or hand block), 120 then 220 grit
- 4+ clamps (bar or F-clamps, at least 12" reach)
- Tape measure, combination square, pencil
- Wood glue brush or scrap applicator

---

## Step-by-Step

### 1. Cut everything to size
Cut all pieces per the cut list above. Square every cut with a combination square before moving to the next — a chair only feels rickety if the legs aren't dead square to the rails. Label pieces with painter's tape as you go (FL = front leg, BL = back leg, etc.) so nothing gets mixed up.

### 2. Build the seat frame
- Lay the 2 front legs and 2 back legs on their sides.
- Attach the front rail (13") between the two front legs: top edge of rail flush with a mark 17" up from the floor-end of the front legs. Glue the joint, clamp, then drive two 2.5" screws per side (pre-drill a 1/8" pilot hole through the leg into the rail end, countersink).
- Attach the back rail (13") between the two back legs the same way, at the same 17"-from-floor height (measuring up from the bottom of the back legs).
- Attach the two side rails (12") connecting front leg to back leg on each side, same height, same method. This gives you a complete box frame standing on 4 legs.

### 3. Add the lower stretchers
- Attach the front stretcher (13"), back stretcher (13"), and two side stretchers (12") about 4" up from the floor, same glue + screw method as the rails. These keep the legs from racking side to side — don't skip them.

### 4. Attach the seat slats
- Lay the 3 seat slats (15" each) across the seat frame, running front-to-back, resting on top of the front and back rails.
- Space them evenly across the 16" width (roughly 3/4" gap between slats, similar overhang at front/back).
- Screw each slat down into the front and back rails with two 1.25" screws per end (pilot hole first, pine splits easily near the ends).

### 5. Attach the backrest
- Mark the back legs at 24" and 32" up from the floor (measuring on the inside face).
- Attach the two backrest slats (13" each) at those marks, glue + two 1.25" screws per end into each back leg.

### 6. Sand
- 120 grit first to knock down mill marks and round over sharp edges, then 220 grit for a smooth final pass. Pay extra attention to the seat slats and backrest — those are the surfaces that'll touch skin.

### 7. Fill and finish
- Fill exposed screw heads with wood filler if you want a cleaner look, let dry, sand flush.
- Wipe down, then apply stain/poly or paint in thin coats per the can's instructions. Let cure fully (usually 24 hours) before sitting in it.

---

## Notes
- This design skips any back rake/lean angle to keep every cut a simple square crosscut. It'll be less lounge-comfortable than a angled-back chair, but it's stable, fast to build, and forgiving of small measurement errors — a solid first chair build.
- Once you've built one and want to level up, the next version worth trying is the same frame with the back legs and backrest slats cut at a slight back angle (a miter saw makes that cut far more repeatable than a circular saw).
